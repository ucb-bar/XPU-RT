The short version

The runtime is not the problem. The cost model is — and only for small tiles.

The headline number from this sweep was that measured makespans run about 17% longer than predicted. It would be easy to read that as runtime overhead — thread wake-ups, gate jitter, dispatch cost. The traces say otherwise, and the decomposition is unusually clean.

Summed tile execution time runs 1.147× its prediction, while the makespan runs 1.115×. The two track each other point for point. Meanwhile the runtime's own dispatch latency is 8.6 µs — about 1% of a median tile. Essentially all of the gap is the cost model under-predicting how long tiles take, not the machinery that runs them.

And that error is not spread evenly. It is almost entirely concentrated in tiles the model predicts at under a millisecond, which over-run by a roughly fixed amount rather than a proportional one. Large accelerator tiles — the ones that actually dominate these schedules — predict to within a fraction of a percent.

Prediction accuracy

Two regimes, and they behave completely differently

Twelve points mix periodic control models with heavy non-periodic vision work. Six are purely periodic. Splitting on that line separates the sweep into two populations that barely overlap.

All points18 1.115×1.000 – 1.250 —

Mean 1.123×, standard deviation 0.082. Three of the six periodic-only points land at exactly 1.000×.

Those 1.000× points are worth pausing on. Where a schedule is release-gated — the next instance cannot start until its wall-clock release time regardless of how fast the hardware is — the runtime hits its targets to the microsecond and the model is exact by construction. Error appears only where the schedule becomes compute-bound. The two periodic-only points that are compute-bound come in at 1.090× and 1.130×, right in line with the mixed population.

So the 17% is not a uniform modelling bias smeared across every schedule. It is concentrated entirely in the compute-bound portions, and the release-gated portions contribute none of it.

The size effect

Small tiles are over-run by a fixed cost, not a proportional one

Plotting every cell's error against its predicted duration on log axes makes the structure obvious, and it is not noise: the correlation between log predicted duration and log ratio is r = −0.579.

● tiles predicted under 1 ms  ·  ● tiles predicted at 1 ms or more. The dashed horizontal line is a perfect prediction; the vertical line is the 1 ms split.

Tile size classCellsMedian ratioMedian absolute miss
Predicted < 1 ms81.655×+0.234 ms
Predicted ≥ 1 ms100.999×−0.017 ms

A multiplicative cost model cannot express this. The fix is structural, not a matter of re-measuring cells: add a fixed per-dispatch offset of roughly 0.234 ms to every cell, then re-fit the multiplicative part. The large accelerator tiles already sit at 0.99–1.00× and would barely move.

Where does 0.234 ms come from? Not the runtime — measured dispatch latency is 8.6 µs, nearly thirty times smaller. It sits inside the QNN execute call, which is what you would expect if the cost cells were measured in a warm loop where per-invocation setup amortises away, then executed single-shot in situ where it does not.

Lane balance

One lane is saturated, one is nearly idle

dsp 92.5%
hta 56.2%
cpu 26.3%

Median occupancy — lane busy time divided by makespan — across all 18 points. Combined three-lane utilization is 60.8%.

The Hexagon DSP is the bottleneck in every mixed point, sitting between 89% and 99% occupied while the CPU lane idles at a quarter. That asymmetry explains why optimistic cost cells hurt disproportionately: an under-predicted DSP tile makes the solver pack an already-saturated lane tighter than the hardware can sustain, and the overflow has nowhere to go.

It also points at the clearest available headroom. Moving work off DSP onto the idle CPU lane is the single largest structural gain on the table — and the placement data shows the solver already reaches for it when it has to.

The interesting behaviour is what happens under pressure. A yolov8n_head normally prefers DSP but spills onto the CPU lane when DSP is full — which happens in 8 of the 10 points without ViNT. Add ViNT, whose decoder composes on nothing but the CPU, and that spilling stops completely: 0 of 2. The solver reserves the CPU lane for the only tile that can use it.

That is a capability effect on placement, not a magnitude effect on the critical path. It reproduces cleanly and is arguably the most robust structural finding in the sweep.

Every one of the six capped points has 25 operations or more; every point at 23 or fewer solved to proven optimality, most in under three seconds.

The cliff is sharp and sits right around 25–30 operations. Below it the MILP is nearly free; above it the 300 s limit binds every time. Since the capped solutions still produced the best measured makespans in the sweep, the practical cost of the cap looks low — but it does mean the largest points are not proven optima, and any claim of optimality there should be hedged.

The r = +0.992 between predicted and actual is the reassuring one: the model ranks and scales schedules correctly even while it mis-levels them. For choosing between candidate schedules — which is what the scheduler is actually for — that matters more than the absolute offset.

The +0.592 between makespan and ratio is the caution. Error compounds with schedule length, so the 17% median is optimistic for the largest workloads and pessimistic for the smallest.

A result that reversed

The mechanism is not that fused_split dispatches more slowly — it is simply more work. One point executes 13.9 ms of busy time against its baseline's 4.9 ms inside the same 20 ms release window. So the real question is whether that extra work fits in the schedule's slack, and the answer depends entirely on what sets the makespan: release-gated schedules hide it, compute-bound ones expose it, and a dominant yolov8n_head masks it altogether.

Across all three regimes, fused_full's advantage does not reproduce on this target, and where it is visible at all it is a cost. The FPGA result this sweep was designed to mirror does not carry over.

Instrumentation defects found

Two bugs the aggregate analysis exposed

  1. Trace CSV corruption from stream interleaving — 2 of 54 runs. The capture ran the board process under ssh … > run.log 2>&1, merging the streams locally — so ssh carried remote stdout and stderr as two independent channels and interleaved them on arrival, dropping a progress line into the middle of a CSV row. Reported makespans were unaffected (they parse the summary line, which stayed intact), but derived traces were. Fixed on both sides: line-buffered stdout in the emitted runtime so a record is never split, and merging stderr into stdout on the board so ssh carries one ordered stream. Verified clean on 6 of 6 fresh runs.
  2. Periodic instance counts silently collapsed. With no non-periodic work, workload_factory.py reset every periodic network to a single instance while ignoring an explicit num_instances — a 12-operation workload was scheduled as 3. This is what made the six periodic-only points look unschedulable in the first place. Fixed; a regression check re-solves an untouched point to a bit-identical 67.571 ms.

What to do with this

Four changes, in order of expected return

  • Add a fixed per-dispatch offset to the cost model (~0.234 ms), then re-fit. This is the single highest-value change. It targets the one structural error in the model and leaves the already-accurate large-tile cells essentially untouched.
  • Re-measure cost cells single-shot rather than in a warm loop. The offset above is a correction for a measurement-methodology mismatch; fixing the methodology addresses the cause rather than the symptom.
  • Rebalance away from the DSP. At 92.5% median occupancy against a 26.3% CPU lane, the DSP is where every scheduling error becomes visible. This is also where the GPU lane, already demonstrated as a genuine fourth lane, would pay off most.
  • Report optimality honestly above ~25 operations. Six of 18 points hit the 300 s cap and are optimal_inaccurate. Either raise the limit for large points or stop describing those solutions as optimal.
